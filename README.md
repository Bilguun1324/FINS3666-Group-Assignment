# WBTC/WETH MEV Arbitrage Notebook Project

This project is now notebook-only for submission. The computational code artifact is a single Jupyter notebook, with no dependency on a local Python package such as `src/mev_dataset` or separate helper `.py` files.

The project studies historical cross-DEX arbitrage between the `WBTC/WETH` pools on:

- `Uniswap V2`
- `SushiSwap V2`

The goal is not to execute live trades. The goal is to reconstruct historical pool state from on-chain events, simulate hypothetical arbitrage trades, and measure when a price inefficiency would have been large enough to produce positive economic value.

## High-Level Workflow

The project workflow is:

1. Collect or load raw Ethereum event logs for the two pools.
2. Decode the logs into readable event tables.
3. Reconstruct pool reserves over time.
4. Forward-fill those reserves at `1-second` frequency.
5. Compute the implied pool price in each DEX.
6. Simulate a hypothetical arbitrage trade between the two DEXs.
7. Compare gross edge, fees, gas, and final net profitability.
8. Summarize the results and test how the answer changes for different `WBTC` trade sizes.

The key design choice is that the second-level dataset is built by carrying forward the latest observed pool reserves between on-chain `Sync` events. That means one state can persist across many seconds. A run of positive seconds should therefore be interpreted as one persistent opportunity window, not as many independent real trades.

## How Arbitrage Profit Is Defined Here

This project models cross-DEX arbitrage as:

1. buy `WBTC` in the cheaper pool using `WETH`
2. sell that `WBTC` into the more expensive pool
3. compare what comes back against what was spent

The notebook simulates this using constant-product AMM math. It does not just compare two displayed prices.

That distinction matters because a visible price difference is not the same thing as executable profit.

## What Could Make Positive Money

Positive money comes from a sufficiently large temporary mispricing between the two pools.

In practical terms, positive value appears when:

- one DEX is pricing `WBTC` meaningfully cheaper than the other
- the trade is small enough that slippage does not erase the spread
- the gross round-trip edge is larger than all trading costs

So the economic driver is:

`cross-DEX price difference > slippage + DEX fees + gas`

If that inequality holds, the arbitrage is positive.

## What Costs Destroy Profit

There are three main costs.

### 1. Slippage / Price Impact

This is usually the most important cost in AMM arbitrage.

When the trade size is large relative to pool reserves:

- buying moves the cheap pool price up
- selling moves the expensive pool price down

That means the trade worsens its own opportunity while it is being executed. Even if the two pools look mispriced before the trade, the trade itself can consume the edge.

### 2. DEX Fees

Each side of the round trip pays liquidity-provider fees. Since arbitrage requires one trade in each pool, the fee burden is paid twice.

### 3. Gas

Even if the gross trading edge is positive, Ethereum transaction costs can turn the trade negative after execution.

## Why The Notebook Calculates Price From Reserves

The notebook does not rely only on observed swap prices because swap prices are trade-specific. A swap price depends on the exact size of that observed swap.

For arbitrage analysis, we need the current pool state and then must simulate a new hypothetical trade size. That requires current reserves, not just the price of the last observed swap.

So the notebook:

- reconstructs reserves from events
- derives the implied pool price
- simulates execution for the chosen `WBTC` size

That is the correct approach for executable arbitrage analysis.

## Why A Price Difference Does Not Guarantee Profit

A simple price gap between Uniswap and SushiSwap is only the starting point.

A trade is only truly profitable if the price gap survives:

- your own trade’s slippage
- DEX fees
- gas costs

This is why the project includes both:

- net opportunity labeling
- gross size-sensitivity analysis

The first asks, “Would this actually make money after costs?”

The second asks, “Was there at least an exploitable pricing inefficiency before fees and gas?”

## Interpreting Positive Opportunity Counts

The notebook can count positive seconds, but those counts must be interpreted carefully.

A positive second means:

- under the modeled reserve state at that timestamp
- a hypothetical arbitrage of the specified size
- would have had positive gross or net edge

It does not mean a real bot definitely executed at that second.

Also, consecutive positive seconds often represent one continuous market condition rather than many separate opportunities. For that reason, the notebook also groups consecutive positive seconds into opportunity windows.

## Most Important Economic Insight From This Project

The key result is usually not whether there was any visible price difference. There often is.

The more important question is whether the difference is large enough relative to pool depth.

In this project, the main economic pattern is:

- small `WBTC` trade sizes can sometimes exploit the mispricing
- large `WBTC` trade sizes usually cannot

That means the market inefficiencies are economically real but shallow. They exist, but they do not support large trades before slippage destroys them.

## Expected Interpretation Of The Trade-Size Sensitivity Section

The size-sensitivity section exists to answer exactly this question:

> At what `WBTC` size does the opportunity stop being economically viable, even before fees and gas?

If small sizes show many gross-positive seconds while larger sizes show few or none, then the conclusion is:

- there were pricing inefficiencies
- those inefficiencies were small
- pool depth was not large enough to support bigger arbitrage trades

This is often the cleanest explanation for why a dataset can show visible mispricing but still show zero large-scale profitable arbitrage.
